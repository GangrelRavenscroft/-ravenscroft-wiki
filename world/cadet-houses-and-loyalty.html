Established World-Wide Principles

A House must remain worth belonging to.

The Great Houses govern their cadets through both lawful constitutional authority and unequal but often rewarding webs of kinship, property, prestige, education and trust. The methods vary because the Houses themselves are sovereign, culturally distinct and politically competitive.

01

A distinction of rank and sovereignty

Cadet Houses, recognized Major Houses, patroned families, collateral blood households and separate Great Houses are different political and constitutional categories. A cadet House is a distinct, internally governed subordinate dynasty, with its own Head, succession, private resources, officers and dependent networks, operating much like a Major House under the recognized Great House's overarching dynastic authority. Its common bloodline provides additional family privileges and obligations. A collateral member of the parent family does not automatically govern a cadet House. Independent Major Houses, bound Major Houses, and protected or allied Lesser Houses must each retain their proper distinct relationships.

Cadet Houses may become very wealthy and influential while remaining constitutionally subordinate, but shared blood does not automatically confer senior-line succession, a Conclave Voice or unrestricted Order clearance. See the wider Great-House Power Structure for relationships with industry, governments, finance, corporate structures and covert organizations.

02

The public constitutional reform

After the Valdieri civil war (1934–1946), the Conclave adopted the Articles of Dynastic Continuity in 1953–1956. Succession authenticity, cadet charters, verified emergency authority and institutional continuity became shared requirements, without giving the Council routine power over internal family law. A former cadet can still earn independent recognition if it demonstrates genuine sovereign qualification.

03

The unspoken political system

Alongside those formal articles, Great Houses cultivate cadet loyalty through a varied combination of family formation, educational advantages, grants of operational responsibility, patronage, favored career and business opportunities, access to common reserves, marriages, social prestige, compartmented security and information, and a strong sense of shared ancestry. These practices form a political culture, not a second universal set of binding Order laws.

04

Economic and professional interests

A House may offer joint capital, expertise, relationships and large-scale protected institutional infrastructure that individual cadets cannot efficiently replicate. Branches should also retain real assets and achievements of their own: a relationship that brings only coercion and no durable benefits would be far less stable.

05

Education and relationships

Interbranch schooling, regular family functions, mentoring, common rites and carefully chosen professional collaboration can create genuine emotional loyalty across generations. Some relationships simultaneously serve diplomatic or political purposes. Not every marriage or childhood friendship is an instrument of House policy.

06

Clearance and power

A cadet's wealth, blood ancestry, marriage, patronage network and formal office do not confer identical rights. Each family distinguishes constitutional succession, branch or House office, and access to classified information or operational authority. A junior member may hold a prestigious profession yet have no knowledge of deeply compartmented historical covert operations.

07

Quiet coercion and exceptions

Some Great Houses employ harsher means of control when challenged. Such conduct is part of their private political history, not an automatic constitutional entitlement. The Genova children's postwar placement in the senior Valdieri household was a particularly coercive settlement. Their formal return between approximately ages 21 and 23 under the Atto di Rientro remains distinct from broader House customs.

08

Different House temperaments

Ravenscroft favors wealth, patronage, opportunity, shared formation and dense relationships; Falkenrath combines hierarchy, tradition and formal branch charters; Wycliffe emphasizes fiduciary power and institutional legitimacy; Veyrholm relies on shared technical and logistics infrastructure; Valdieri emphasizes restored succession order and economic interdependence. The application to other Houses remains to be developed individually.

09

A required part of every House record

Every new Great-House record and substantive revision should describe cadet branches and their resources; succession and charters; public and private incentives for loyalty; treatment of dissent; boundaries of intelligence and office; historical incidents and unresolved grievances. If a House has no established cadets, show the policy and mark the family structure as developing, rather than inventing names.
